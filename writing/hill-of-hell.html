The Trouble of Getting What You Wished For

There is little that sits right in Laura van den Berg’s “Hill of Hell”. We meet a narrator that is hyperaware but tragically passive, in a narrative that is both gruesomely detailed yet somehow spinning out of focus. She is cryptic about how exactly things make her feel and chooses to communicate these emotions by metaphors and vignettes of those around her. The story itself breaks into two unequal halves: the first being a two-hour train ride conversation between the narrator and her friend, and the second a dramatic tour of the subsequent thirty-three years of her life. Our first glimpse into this turbulent internal dialogue happens in the first half, when the narrator tells her friend about the “worst thing that happened to [her] in the last three years” (Van Den Berg 33), which she relays back to the reader via reported indirect speech:

Last September, I told my friend, I was pregnant. My husband had been the one who wracked with longing for a child and I had allowed myself to be carried along by the tide of his enthusiasm, but once it was underway I felt like I had been conned into a heist for which, as the plans came into focus, I was woefully unprepared. You’re talking about robbing the Louvre and I’m just a common criminal! In those early weeks, I willed my body to show up with the getaway car and then six months later, after I had forgotten all about getaway cars, I was standing in an Ikea in Stoughton, of all the undignified places, waving a spatula and lecturing my husband about how our dairy products were teeming with opiates, when my shorts filled with blood and I fainted. While I was unconscious, I had a dream that men in white coats were elbow deep in me and then I awoke in a hospital bed to find a doctor elbow deep in me, working on my body with the patient, grave air of an executioner. The baby had ten fingers and ten toes, the only thing that many a stranger had told me I should care about. Eyelids as thin as organza. (Van Den Berg 34)

This story feels jarring, and not simply from the events themselves or the fact that it is thrust upon us so quickly only two pages into the text. There is an eerie and haunted quality to the phrasing and metaphors; the pacing clashes with the gentle suspended feeling of the scenic train ride along the river. It evokes a feeling of circular modularity that stays with us as we continue through the text, a lingering déjà vu that we cannot shake off. Van den Berg is deliberate in these choices, and she uses careful phrasing, metaphor, and timeline to force the reader to experience the same loss of control and othering that torment the narrator in a story that repeats itself throughout the rest of the piece.

The narrator alienates herself from her own body, using metaphors for her miscarriage to other herself from everything that happens to her. When she first gets pregnant, she hopes to end the pregnancy by willing that her body “show up with a getaway car” (34). She treats it as a separate entity that operates independently of the self that is doing the wishing. When the car appears, she describes the experience of internal bleeding – something fundamentally within the body – as her “shorts fill[ing] with blood”, and the subsequent treatment as a doctor “elbow deep inside me” (34). This choice of removing the body from the subject of these sentences displaces it into a reference frame opposite to us, as something external that doesn’t do things itself but rather has things done to it. The doctor himself is psychologized with the “patient, grave air of an executioner” who is “working on [her] body” (34), a gruesome description that elicits images of a butcher carving away at a piece of meat. We cannot help but feel complicit as we are confronted with this series of otherings that emerge one after another, each new metaphor spilling over into the next.

The use of metaphors doesn’t end there; not only does the narrator use them to other herself from her own body, but also to demonstrate her lack of control over her own narrative. When she first mentions her pregnancy, she does this by analog of a shipwreck: her husband is “wracked with longing for a child” and she lets herself be “carried along by the tide of his enthusiasm” (34). In all this she maintains a passive voice, swept up by the actions and feelings of those around her. Her husband, despite this influence, is never mentioned by name in the entire story. In fact, the narrator keeps all the surrounding cast of characters around her nameless, resulting in a smeared and blurred together quality to everyone around her. If this metaphor for miscarriage weren’t uncomfortable enough, she proceeds to demonstrate the gravity of her situation by comparing it to “robbing the Louvre” as “just a common criminal” (34), a highly unusual way to portray her realization of the responsibilities of motherhood. This mention of the Louvre by name is also deliberate, simultaneously anchoring us to a specific location and further emphasizing how indistinguishable and grey all other names and faces in the text are.

Yet despite this apparent passivity, the narrator also experiences a deep guilt for wishing that certain events transpire. The only times in which she jolts out of her passivity are when she does this wishing or when these wishes are granted. “I willed” (34), she says when talking about wanting a miscarriage, switching to active voice. Later, she is unable to let this guilt go, gravitating back to it again and again throughout the rest of the piece. When confronting her second daughter’s addiction, she muses that “one must be very careful about the desperate wishes cast out into the ether because perhaps someone is listening, someone all too willing to grant us exactly what we have asked for and maybe even what we deserve” (38).

With these metaphors in place, Van Den Berg proceeds to construct a timeline in this excerpt that directly mirrors that of the overall story, causing the reader to experience the same circularity of fate that traps the narrator. The image of doctors “elbow deep inside me” (34) is repeated twice, first as a dream and then as cruel reality when the narrator regains consciousness. The excerpt starts out slow, spending the first three sentences discussing the early days of the pregnancy. By the fourth, we are pushed back against our seats as the narrator takes us from the slow account of wishful thinking to the end scene of the miscarriage itself. This same jump from slow beginning to violent end is true of the story as a whole, with the first half of the piece having the suspended-in-time quality of a train ride along the Hudson that halfway through morphs into a whirlwind tour of thirty-three turbulent years of life.

In fact, this choice of tempo reinforces the overall nesting doll construction of this narration: the story as a whole – of a mother losing her daughter – is contained within this one excerpt. We feel this in the eerie mirrored depictions of the narrator’s two children, both of whom we are introduced to only in death. In this excerpt, the narrator’s only mention of her first child comes after the work of the “executioner” (34) is complete. We similarly know little of the narrator’s second daughter until “dying the best death possible [becomes] her full-time job” (39). We get few physical descriptions of either child, but the narrator spends time lingering on the details of both their eyes, mentioning the first daughter’s “eyelids as thin as organza” (34) and feeling the second daughter’s “lashes brush against [her] skin” (41). Opiates are also mentioned in tandem with both children. For the first child, the miscarriage comes while the narrator is lecturing her husband on how “dairy products were teeming with opiates” (34), and the second daughter’s early life – the life she led before she becomes the character that departs – revolved around opiates and addiction (37-8). Yet in both cases opiates are insignificant in the context of the broader causes of both their deaths. The narrator wonders whether her first child was reborn in the form of her second one, whether she has been “doomed to relive the same story, with the same ending” (40). The final scene we are left with is one of heartbreak an exhaustion, with the narrator wondering “if nature loves symmetry then why is symmetry so cruel?” (42), voicing this theme outright.

The mirrored effect of this excerpt that contains the broader story within it reinforces the circularity of the narrative as a whole. The metaphors and word choice are intended to make us as readers feel uncomfortable. We experience the same nauseating and disorienting sensation that the narrator endures throughout her life, of both her guilt over wishing things that are fundamentally misfortunate, and the lack of control and agency she has once these wishes are willed. Van den Berg is very deliberate in this construction: throughout the piece she very carefully stitches together sequences of events that have subconscious continuity but don’t quite fit together. The result is an extremely physical, gripping, and disjoint narrative from which we emerge exhausted, lying in the hospital bed alongside the narrator and the nameless second husband beside her.
